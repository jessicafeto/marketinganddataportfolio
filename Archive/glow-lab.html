CRM Segmentation & Influencer Marketing Strategy for an Indie Beauty Brand

A self-directed portfolio project: a fictional DTC skincare brand, a synthetic 12-month dataset of customers and campaigns, and a full marketing & CRM strategy built from the data up.

✦ Glow Lab is a fictional brand. All customer and campaign data is synthetic, generated to demonstrate this analysis & strategy approach.
01 — The Brief

A strong launch, then a plateau

Glow Lab is a UK-based indie skincare brand. The premise: it launched in January 2025 with a splashy macro-influencer campaign that drove a strong first month of sales. Over the following year, the team scaled up paid social advertising to keep growth going — and by the end of the year, the founders are asking three questions:

1. Why is acquisition getting more expensive?

It feels like the brand is spending more to acquire the same number of customers it used to acquire more cheaply.

2. Is a 26% repeat rate normal?

Most customers buy once and never come back. Is that just how skincare works, or is it a problem worth solving?

3. Did that influencer campaign work?

The January launch felt like a success — but was it actually good value compared to other channels?

My brief: analyse a year of customer and campaign data, answer those three questions with evidence, and turn the answers into a concrete marketing and CRM strategy for the next 6 months.

02 — The Data & Approach

How I built and analysed the dataset

To make this project realistic, I built a synthetic dataset that mirrors what a DTC beauty brand's data actually looks like, then analysed it in Python.

The dataset

1,170 customers acquired across 4 channels (Influencer – Macro, Influencer – Micro, Paid Social, Organic & Referral) over 12 months, generating 1,552 orders. Repeat-purchase timing reflects a realistic skincare replenishment cycle (roughly 6–13 weeks between orders), with repeat likelihood varying by acquisition channel. 36 monthly campaign records track spend, conversions and revenue across the three paid channels — with Paid Social CAC deliberately rising across the year, a pattern common to DTC brands as they scale up paid spend.

The analysis

Using pandas and numpy, I ran an RFM-style segmentation (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) to group customers into four actionable personas, calculated CAC and ROAS by channel and month, measured repeat purchase rate by acquisition channel, and split monthly revenue between new and returning customers. The results feed directly into the dashboard below.

All data, the generation script and the analysis notebook are on GitHub: github.com/jessicafeto/glow-lab-marketing-analytics

Finding 1 — acquisition is getting more expensive, but only on Paid Social. Paid Social CAC more than doubled over the year, from £14.49 in January to £36.35 in December, while Influencer – Macro (~£9–13) and Influencer – Micro (~£5–7) stayed flat. By December, Paid Social costs roughly 3x what Influencer – Macro costs, and 6x what Influencer – Micro costs.

Finding 2 — Influencer – Micro is the standout channel on every metric. It returns £5.13 for every £1 spent (vs. £2.65 for Influencer – Macro and just £1.22 for Paid Social — barely covering ad spend before product cost, on a first-order basis). Customers acquired via Influencer – Micro and Organic & Referral also repeat at ~37–38%, more than 2.5x the rate of Paid-Social-acquired customers (14.2%). Channel choice isn't just shaping acquisition cost — it's shaping who becomes a loyal customer.

Finding 3 — most revenue still comes from one-time buyers, and the repeat engine is barely warmed up. 867 customers (74.1% of the base) have ordered exactly once, but still account for 54.2% of revenue simply because there are so many of them. The 172 "At Risk" customers (14.7%) used to be repeat buyers but haven't ordered in 120+ days — they still represent 25.9% of revenue. Returning-customer revenue grew from £0 in January to a fairly steady £1,000–£1,700/month by mid-year, but it's still a small share of the monthly total. There's clear headroom here.

04 — Customer Segments

Four personas, four different plans

I grouped customers using Recency (days since last order), Frequency (number of orders) and Monetary value (total spend) — a classic RFM approach, simplified into four personas the marketing team can plan around.

VIP Glow Girls

29 customers (2.5%) · avg LTV £112.91 · 3.3 orders avg

Your most loyal customers — 3+ orders, ordered recently. They already love the brand.

Action: Referral & ambassador programme Give them an incentive to bring friends — this is the cheapest possible acquisition channel, and it feeds the Influencer – Micro pipeline.

Repeat Regulars

102 customers (8.7%) · avg LTV £67.95 · 2.0 orders avg

On their way to becoming VIPs — they've already come back once.

Action: Replenishment + "level up" flow Subscribe-and-save offers and reorder reminders timed to their first reorder gap, nudging toward a 3rd order.

One-and-Done

867 customers (74.1%) · avg LTV £31.96 · 1 order

The biggest group by far — they bought once and never returned. The single biggest growth lever.

Action: Post-purchase nurture flow Usage tips, ingredient education, and a replenishment reminder timed to the product's ~6–8 week runout.

At Risk

172 customers (14.7%) · avg LTV £77.03 · 2.2 orders avg

Used to be repeat buyers, then went quiet (120+ days). They've already proven they'll buy more than once.

Action: Win-back campaign "We've missed you" messaging, new product launches, and a time-limited incentive to bring them back.
05 — Strategy & Recommendations

Three moves for the next 6 months

1. Rebalance acquisition spend

Paid Social drives 45% of new customers but has the worst ROAS (1.22x) and lowest repeat rate (14.2%). Cap Paid Social spend at current levels and redirect incremental budget into an always-on Influencer – Micro / ambassador programme, where ROAS is over 4x higher and repeat rate is 2.6x higher.

2. Build a segment-based CRM programme

Give each persona its own lifecycle flow: a post-purchase nurture series for One-and-Done customers, a replenishment + "level up" series for Repeat Regulars, a win-back series for At Risk customers, and a referral/ambassador invite for VIP Glow Girls — each timed to the ~6–8 week skincare replenishment cycle.

3. Restructure the influencer programme

Use 1–2 macro-influencer placements per year for big moments (launch, Black Friday — both already drove acquisition spikes in this data), but build an always-on roster of 8–12 micro-influencers/ambassadors for ongoing content and acquisition, where cost-per-acquisition is lowest and repeat rate is highest.

08 — Projected Impact

What this could be worth

A rough, illustrative model of the upside from the three recommendations above.

+£10,660 Reallocating ~20% of Paid Social spend (~£2,730/yr) to Influencer – Micro at its 5.1x ROAS vs. Paid Social's 1.2x
+£4,680 If 15% of the 867 One-and-Done customers convert to a 2nd order via the post-purchase nurture flow (~£36 AOV)
+£1,220 If 20% of the 172 At Risk customers reactivate with one more order via the win-back flow (~£36 AOV)

Combined, these three initiatives could lift the overall repeat purchase rate from 25.9% to roughly 37%, and add approximately £16,500 (~32%) on top of the £51,163 baseline — within about 6 months of consistent execution.

A note on these numbers: these are illustrative projections based on the stated assumptions (conversion benchmarks for post-purchase email/SMS flows, and an assumed linear ROAS for incremental influencer spend) — they're meant to show how I'd frame a recommendation with a number attached, not a guarantee. In a real engagement, I'd validate these assumptions against the brand's actual flow performance and test incrementally (e.g. via holdout groups) before scaling any budget shifts.
09 — Behind the Scenes

Tools & methodology

How this was built

  • Synthetic data generated in Python (pandas, numpy) with reproducible random seeding
  • RFM segmentation, CAC/ROAS and cohort analysis in pandas
  • Dashboard built with HTML, CSS and Chart.js
  • Full code and data on GitHub for transparency

Why I built it this way

I wanted a project that shows the full pipeline I'd bring to a marketing or data role: defining the business question, building/analysing the data, translating findings into a strategy non-technical stakeholders can act on, and putting a number on the recommendation. It's the same approach I used in my MSc dissertation on data-driven fashion analytics — applied here to beauty.
